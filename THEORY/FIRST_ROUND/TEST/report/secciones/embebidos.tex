\subsection{Effect on the sampling period}

Mechanism 4(a) is the only one of the three whose effective sampling period scales almost linearly with $N$: it grows from $150$ ms ($N=10^6$) to $600$ ms ($N=10^7$) to $5100$ ms ($N=10^8$), far from the nominal $100$ ms period, because the summation and sampling share the same blocking thread. Mechanism 4(b) keeps the nominal period essentially constant across all three values of $N$ ($100.4$/$99.8$/$100.0$ ms, individual samples ranging only $98$–$102$ ms), because the sampling task runs on a core physically separate from the summation. Mechanism 4(c) keeps the hardware timer's timing reference exact — seven of the eight printed samples per run stay near $100$ ms in every capture — but one sample per run at $N=10^7$ and $N=10^8$ shows \texttt{loop()} catching up on an accumulated backlog: an important distinction between \emph{when the interrupt fires} (exact) and \emph{when \texttt{loop()} manages to process it} (delayed if the summation is long).

\subsection{Which mechanism is more precise?}

Based on the captured data, the ranking of \emph{achieved sampling period} precision (not just timing reference) is:

\begin{equation}
\text{4(b) dual core} \;>\; \text{4(c) interrupt} \;>\; \text{4(a) busy loop}
\end{equation}

The \textbf{dual-core mechanism (4b)} is the most precise in practical terms: by running sampling and the compute load on physically separate cores, the effective sampling period stays close to nominal regardless of $N$ — the tightest and most consistent result across all three values of $N$ in the captured data. The \textbf{interrupt mechanism (4c)} offers the most exact hardware-level timing reference of the three — the timer fires exactly every $100$ ms regardless of what \texttt{loop()} is doing — but because \texttt{loop()} itself must process each sample (read the potentiometers and print), a sufficiently long summation ($N=10^7$, $N=10^8$) causes processing to fall behind and several interrupts to accumulate before being serviced, degrading the \emph{observed} precision even though the timer's own firing is unaffected. The \textbf{busy loop (4a)} is unambiguously the least precise: the blocking summation directly delays every sample, proportionally to $N$, with no mechanism preventing it — exactly as measured.

In summary: if the requirement is a constant, predictable sampling rate independent of compute load, the dual-core mechanism is the most robust choice; if the requirement is an exact timing reference with some tolerance for delayed processing of individual events (not delayed triggering), the hardware interrupt is preferable to the busy loop, and both alternatives avoid entirely the linear degradation suffered by the single-threaded mechanism.
