\section{Introduction}

Sampling, quantization, and spectral analysis are fundamental operations when converting an analog signal into a processable digital representation. Sampling determines how faithfully a continuous signal can be reconstructed from its discrete samples; an insufficient sampling rate introduces \emph{aliasing}, a phenomenon in which high-frequency components are misrepresented as low-frequency ones. Quantization, in turn, approximates each sample to one of a finite number of levels, introducing a quantization error inherent to the analog-to-digital/digital-to-analog conversion process (ADC/DAC). Finally, the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) allows the frequency content of an already-digitized signal to be studied, useful both for characterizing synthetic signals and for differentiating real sound sources.

This work addresses these concepts through four independent exercises, implemented in the \texttt{signal\_tools} package of the \texttt{TEST} project: (1) analysis of a real sampled signal (\texttt{Muestra01.csv}); (2) sampling and quantization of a reference sine wave at three different sampling rates, directly illustrating aliasing; (3) spectral comparison of three real sound sources of very different nature (a string instrument, a percussion instrument, and an animal sound); and (4) an analysis of the timing precision achievable when sampling analog sensors on an ESP32-WROOM microcontroller under three different concurrency mechanisms, against a compute load competing for the same processor.

The objective is twofold: on one hand, to apply and experimentally verify (through code and real data, not only theory) the concepts of sampling, aliasing, and FFT; on the other, to extend the analysis to an embedded-systems engineering case where the \emph{timing precision of sampling} — not just its nominal frequency — depends critically on the chosen concurrency mechanism.
